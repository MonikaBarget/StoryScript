Unifying the German Currency System


Issuing currencies and minting coins within their own dominions was one of the fundamental rights of European princes. While we are now used to one currency per nation state, or even per transnational monetary union in the case of the Euro Zone, coinage regulations were more complicated in the past. In the Holy Roman Empire, the right to mint coins Münzrecht, was granted by the emperor to individual feudal princes as well as cities. In the 16th century, the Empire no longer minted coins itself but aimed to issue general minting regulations. The Reichsmüntzordnung of 1559, however, faced resistance, notably from Westphalia and Burgundy. Historian Hans-Wolfgang Bergerhausen (1993) explores this struggle in his book Kampf um die Reichsmünzordnung. The mid-17th century saw several armed conflicts across Europe, among them the infamous Thirty Years' War that also came with famine and plagues, leaving many regions in present-day Germany impoverished and depopulated. This also had an impact on the German currency system. By 1679, the circles of Franconia, Bavaria, and Swabia sought to restore currency stability through collaboration. The first country in Europe to establish a Central Bank was Sweden who founded the Sveriges Riksbankin 1668. Question: What policy did Westphalia and Burgundy adopt?
